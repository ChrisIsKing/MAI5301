### Problem Addressed and Problem Importance
Large language models and the software built on top of them could reshape a wide range of occupations, yet prior exposure studies focused narrowly on machine learning or automation potential and often missed the complementary technologies that amplify impact.

### State of Related Works in This Topic
Earlier task-exposure work (Brynjolfsson et al., Felten et al., Webb) mapped AI capabilities to jobs but did not incorporate real LLM performance or the rapid development of LLM-powered tools. Studies of general-purpose technologies existed but lacked concrete measurements for LLMs.

### Proposed Solution
The authors develop a new rubric to assess how much of each O*NET task could be done by LLMs alone versus LLM-powered software. Using both human annotators and GPT-4 itself, they label ~19% of U.S. jobs as having at least 50% of tasks exposed to current LLMs; the figure rises to 47–56% when complementary tooling is included. They show exposure spans all wage levels (higher-income jobs more exposed) and is not concentrated in high-productivity-growth industries.

### Drawbacks and Limitations
The analysis measures technical feasibility rather than actual adoption or labor-displacement outcomes. It is a snapshot based on 2023 capabilities and does not forecast timelines or new job creation.

### Future Research
Tracking exposure over time as models and tools evolve, incorporating additional modalities (images, audio), and examining how LLMs affect task creation and skill demand in the long run.
