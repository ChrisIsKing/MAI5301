The training configuration for the GPT model was designed to balance computational efficiency with learning stability. The architecture followed the GPT-2 124M structure, using 12 transformer layers, 12 attention heads, and an embedding dimension of 768. The context length was reduced from the original 1024 tokens to a smaller value (256–384) to lower memory usage and computational cost while still preserving meaningful context learning. Dropout was kept low (0.0–0.1) due to the small dataset size, and QKV bias was enabled to ensure compatibility with pretrained GPT-2 weights. The AdamW optimizer was used because it is standard for transformer-based models and provides stable convergence through decoupled weight decay. Learning rates ranged between 3e-4 and 5e-4, slightly higher than large-scale training settings, since the dataset was small and faster convergence was desired. A cosine decay schedule with linear warmup was implemented to prevent early training instability and allow smooth convergence. Additionally, gradient accumulation was incorporated to simulate a larger effective batch size without increasing memory requirements, which is particularly important when training on limited hardware.

During training, the loss decreased in a predictable pattern. Initially, the model’s loss was close to the theoretical baseline of a random predictor, approximately equal to the natural logarithm of the vocabulary size (around 10.8 for GPT-2’s vocabulary). In the early training phase, the loss dropped rapidly as the model began learning basic token frequencies, positional patterns, and short-range dependencies. In the mid-phase, the loss continued decreasing more gradually as the model captured syntactic structure and recurring stylistic patterns in the dataset. Toward the later stages, the loss began to plateau, indicating that the model had extracted most of the learnable structure from the limited dataset. In some cases, validation loss increased slightly relative to training loss, signaling mild overfitting. Correspondingly, perplexity, which is the exponential of the loss, decreased as the model improved its predictive confidence.

The quality of generated text improved noticeably throughout training. Before training, the randomly initialized model produced incoherent and grammatically unstable output. After several epochs of training from scratch, the model began generating more structured sentences and demonstrated improved local coherence. It learned recurring vocabulary and stylistic elements from the dataset and produced more readable continuations. However, its capabilities remained limited due to the small training corpus. The scratch-trained model showed tendencies toward repetition, lacked broader semantic understanding, and did not exhibit real-world knowledge. Sampling strategies such as temperature scaling and top-k filtering further influenced generation quality: lower temperatures produced more focused and coherent outputs, while higher temperatures increased diversity but sometimes reduced grammatical stability.

Training from scratch presented several challenges compared to using pretrained weights. The dataset used for scratch training was extremely small relative to the billions of tokens typically required for large language models. As a result, the model learned only shallow structural patterns rather than deep semantic representations. Convergence was slower, overfitting occurred more easily, and the model lacked general knowledge. In contrast, pretrained GPT-2 models were trained on approximately 40GB of internet text using substantial computational resources. These pretrained models already encode grammar, factual knowledge, and contextual reasoning abilities. This demonstrates the importance of pretraining: it dramatically reduces the computational burden required to achieve high-quality language generation and allows fine-tuning to adapt the model efficiently to specific tasks.

Regarding compute resources, the scratch-trained model required relatively modest hardware. With a batch size of 2–4 and reduced context length, training could be performed on a single GPU or even a CPU with sufficient memory, though GPU acceleration significantly improved speed. Memory usage during a forward pass depends primarily on batch size, context length, and embedding dimension. Although the 124M parameter model is relatively small by modern standards, attention mechanisms and gradient storage increase actual memory requirements beyond simple parameter size. In contrast, the original GPT-2 pretraining required multiple GPUs running for approximately a week, highlighting the immense computational investment behind pretrained models. This comparison reinforces the central conclusion: while training from scratch demonstrates architectural understanding and learning dynamics, pretrained weights provide practical performance, scalability, and real-world language capability.
