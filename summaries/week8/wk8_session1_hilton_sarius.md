Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models
This study discusses a major challenge with large language models: even while making the models bigger makes them better at many natural language tasks, they still have trouble with more difficult reasoning tasks, including arithmetic word problems, common sense inference, and symbolic manipulation. Standard prompting methods, which show the model a few question answer pairs before asking it to guess the answer, frequently don't work for these tasks since the model can't break difficulties down into smaller steps of reasoning. This issue is significant, as strong reasoning skills are essential for practical uses like automated teaching, interpretability, and dependable decision-making; nevertheless, conventional language model scaling has not produced enough reasoning outcomes.
Previous research in similar fields has investigated several methodologies. Some researchers improved reasoning by fine-tuning models using supervised datasets with explanations or by creating task-specific mechanisms. Some people thought about making natural language explanations or reducing difficulties down into smaller phases with clear intermediate representations. These methods, on the other hand, often needed more training data, custom architectures, or independent verification modules to check answers. Few shot prompting worked for general language activities, but it wasn't enough for more difficult reasoning tasks. People had already suggested utilising natural language rationales in other situations, but chain-of-thought prompting provided a new way to show that it worked across multiple benchmarks.
The main point of the paper is to introduce and evaluate chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, which is a basic but effective way to give the model a few example issues with clear steps of reasoning in the middle. The prompts don't simply provide question-answer pairs; they also show "chains of thought," which are sequences of natural language processes that indicate how to go from the problem to the answer. The main point is that big models (with tens or hundreds of billions of parameters) may naturally come up with coherent steps of reasoning in between when they are given these examples. This feature only works at big scales; smaller models don't get the same benefit. The authors showed big improvements on many reasoning tests by using CoT prompts. For example, a 540B-parameter model with eight CoT examples got the best results on the GSM8K math word problem dataset, even beating fine-tuned GPT-3 with a separate verifier module.
The method has some good points, but it also has some bad ones. One major problem is that chain-of-thought prompting depends a lot on the size of the model. CoT doesn't work well for smaller or mid-sized models since they can't create and describe structured reasoning stages on their own. Also, making logical chains of thought can make the output longer and the inference cost higher. The method can also be sensitive to the choice and order of examples. Lastly, CoT doesn't make sure that all the stages are valid; in certain circumstances, it might give you reasoning that looks good but isn't.
There are several ways that future study could go. One possible way to go is to create ways for smaller or more efficient models to use reasoning examples, maybe by changing the architecture or setting specific training goals. Another approach is to enhance the reliability and accuracy of the reasoning steps that are generated. This can be achieved by incorporating structured neural symbolic reasoning or verification processes. Additionally, the impact of CoT prompting could be broadened by extending it to tasks with extended dependencies, multimodal inputs, or real-world reasoning applications. Finally, it is still an open and crucial subject to grasp the theoretical reasons why reasoning happens at scale and how it connects to model representations.

Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models

The study "Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models" talks about a big problem with how big language models (LLMs) do hard reasoning tasks like word problems and common sense inference. Previous research indicated that employing chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, where models are directed to formulate sequential reasoning prior to delivering a response can enhance performance on these tasks. But CoT with normal greedy decoding only lets the model follow one path of reasoning, even though many problems have more than one correct line of reasoning. This is significant since reasoning problems typically need you to look at a few possible solutions before you find the right one. Making the model stick to one path can make it less accurate and less reliable. Enhancing reasoning in LLMs has significant ramifications for applications where accuracy and dependability are crucial, including instructional resources, scientific inquiry, and automated reasoning frameworks.

Earlier work on this topic looked at ways to improve chain-of-thought prompting and the design of language models. CoT prompting itself was proven to assist LLMs break down hard tasks into easier-to-understand steps, which made them work far better than just predicting the result. This method still used greedy decoding, which means picking the most likely token at each generation step. This can miss other reasonable reasoning paths. Some work has explored using ensembles or extra verification modules, but these frequently need more training or resources and don't use all the different forms of reasoning that the model might come up with.

The main point of this study is a new way to decode termed self-consistency, which is meant to function with chain-of-thought prompting. The model doesn't just greedily create one reasoning chain; it uses stochastic sampling to create a wide range of reasoning paths by doing numerous decoding runs. Every path that was sampled shows a possible sequence of intermediate reasoning processes and a final answer. The idea behind this method is that even while individual reasoning chains may not always be clear or precise, proper answers tend to show up more often across different reasoning paths than wrong ones. The approach finds the most "self-consistent" response by putting all of the samples together and picking the one that gets the most votes (usually by majority or plurality). This ensembling method changes the problem from trusting one line of reasoning to using a group of different reasoning outputs to make things more accurate.

Empirical findings indicate that self-consistency yields significant performance improvements in both arithmetic and commonsense standards. For instance, the accuracy on the GSM8K arithmetic dataset goes up by about 17.9% compared to greedy CoT decoding. Similar big improvements can be seen on SVAMP, AQuA, StrategyQA, and the ARC-challenge benchmark. These enhancements persist even when employing substantial pre-trained models without supplementary fine-tuning.

Self-consistency has certain problems, even though it works. First, it needs a lot more computing power than single-path greedy decoding since it uses a lot of different reasoning processes. This makes inference more expensive and takes longer. Second, the method presupposes that the samples have a good enough representation of the right reasoning path. If the samples aren't very diverse or the model tends to make similar wrong paths, the method can still not work. Lastly, choosing the most common answer makes the model more robust, but it doesn't directly check the soundness of the reasoning steps. Models might still come up with reasonable but wrong reasoning that dominates the sample set.

Future research endeavours may seek to alleviate the sampling burden by creating more efficient sampling or ensembling techniques that preserve variety with a reduced number of samples. Integration with other verification and scoring systems could improve the process of choosing answers by looking at the quality of reasoning chains, not just how often they are used. Another potential avenue is the adaptation of self-consistency to multi-modal reasoning problems or structured reasoning standards, where pathways may not be readily enumerable. Finally, theoretical research to elucidate the rationale and timing of self-consistency, as well as strategies to optimise its advantages across various model scales and domains, could enhance its utility in automated reasoning systems.
