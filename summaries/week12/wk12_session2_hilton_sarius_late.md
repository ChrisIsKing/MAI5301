The paper “On the Measure of Intelligence” by François Chollet examines how intelligence in artificial systems should be defined and evaluated. The author argues that current approaches to measuring artificial intelligence are flawed because they focus mainly on task-specific performance rather than true intelligence. Many modern AI systems achieve impressive results in tasks such as playing games, image recognition, or language processing. However, these successes often depend on large amounts of training data, engineered priors, or narrow problem settings. According to the paper, this does not necessarily demonstrate general intelligence. The central problem addressed is therefore how to properly define and measure intelligence in AI systems. This problem is important because progress in artificial intelligence depends on having reliable benchmarks that reflect genuine cognitive ability rather than memorization or narrow specialization. Without a clear definition and measurement of intelligence, it becomes difficult to compare systems or evaluate real progress toward artificial general intelligence.

The paper reviews existing ideas and related work on intelligence measurement. Historically, intelligence in AI has often been evaluated through task-based benchmarks, where a system is tested on specific problems such as chess, image classification, or question answering. These benchmarks measure skill at a task rather than the ability to learn new skills. Earlier definitions of intelligence, such as the one proposed by Legg and Hutter, describe intelligence as the ability of an agent to achieve goals in a wide range of environments. However, in practice many AI benchmarks still reward systems that rely heavily on training data and prior knowledge provided by developers. As a result, systems may appear highly capable even though they lack true adaptability or general reasoning ability. The paper also discusses psychological perspectives on intelligence, including the distinction between crystallized intelligence (accumulated knowledge) and fluid intelligence (the ability to reason and solve new problems). Chollet argues that current AI evaluations emphasize crystallized skills while neglecting the ability to acquire new skills efficiently.

To address this issue, the paper proposes a new conceptual framework for defining intelligence. Chollet defines intelligence as the efficiency with which an agent can acquire new skills across a wide range of tasks, given limited prior knowledge and experience. This definition is inspired by Algorithmic Information Theory, which provides a way to analyze the complexity of problems and the information required to solve them. The key insight behind the proposal is that intelligence should be measured not by the amount of knowledge a system already has, but by how efficiently it can learn and generalize when faced with new problems. Based on this definition, the paper introduces the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC) benchmark. ARC is designed to evaluate an AI system’s ability to perform abstract reasoning tasks using minimal training examples. The tasks require recognizing patterns, inferring rules, and applying those rules to new situations, which more closely reflects human-like fluid intelligence. The benchmark also incorporates human-like prior knowledge to ensure fair comparisons between humans and AI systems.

Despite its contributions, the proposal has several limitations. One challenge is that defining the correct priors and task distributions for evaluating intelligence is difficult. If the priors embedded in the benchmark are not appropriate, the evaluation may still favor certain systems or approaches. Additionally, ARC tasks may not capture all aspects of intelligence, such as creativity, social reasoning, or real-world decision-making. Another limitation is that solving ARC tasks can be computationally challenging, and current AI systems still struggle with many of the problems included in the benchmark.

Future research could explore improved benchmarks that evaluate broader forms of intelligence and incorporate multiple types of reasoning, learning, and perception. Researchers may also investigate methods for building systems that learn efficiently from limited data, closer to how humans learn new concepts. Another promising direction is developing evaluation frameworks that continuously generate new tasks, preventing models from overfitting to specific benchmarks. These advances could help create more accurate measurements of general intelligence and guide the development of more adaptable and capable AI systems.


The paper “Chatbot Arena: An Open Platform for Evaluating LLMs by Human Preference” addresses the challenge of effectively evaluating large language models (LLMs). As LLMs have become widely used in applications such as question answering, coding assistance, and conversational agents, accurately assessing their performance has become increasingly important. Traditional evaluation methods rely on static benchmarks or automated metrics, but these approaches often fail to capture the subjective and multi-dimensional nature of human language quality. For example, responses may differ in helpfulness, clarity, or creativity even if they are factually correct. The paper therefore addresses the problem of how to evaluate LLM outputs in a way that reflects real human preferences and real-world usage scenarios. This problem is important because reliable evaluation is necessary for improving models, comparing different systems, and ensuring that deployed AI systems align with human expectations.

Prior work in LLM evaluation has largely focused on automated benchmarks and controlled datasets, such as knowledge tests or coding benchmarks. These benchmarks provide standardized comparisons but often suffer from limitations. Models can overfit to known benchmark datasets, and benchmark questions may not reflect the diversity of real-world user queries. Additionally, automated metrics such as accuracy or BLEU scores do not fully capture subjective qualities like helpfulness or conversational quality. Human evaluation is often considered the most reliable approach, but it is expensive, time-consuming, and difficult to scale across many models and prompts. Earlier datasets that collect human interactions with models provide useful information, but they often lack direct comparisons between models or structured preference signals needed to rank them effectively.

To address these challenges, the authors propose Chatbot Arena, an open platform designed to evaluate LLMs through crowdsourced human preference data. In this system, users interact with two anonymous models simultaneously. After submitting a prompt, the platform displays two responses generated by different models, and the user simply chooses which response they prefer. This pairwise comparison method simplifies evaluation because people often find it easier to compare two answers than to assign absolute scores. The collected votes are then used to statistically rank models based on preference data, producing a leaderboard that reflects how well different systems perform in real-world conversations. The key insight behind the solution is that crowdsourced human comparisons across diverse prompts provide a more realistic and scalable evaluation of LLM performance. Because the prompts are generated by real users rather than fixed datasets, the evaluation captures a wide range of topics, languages, and use cases. Over time, the platform has accumulated hundreds of thousands of votes, demonstrating that crowdsourced human feedback can produce reliable and consistent rankings of language models.

Despite its advantages, the proposed approach also has several limitations. Crowdsourced evaluation may introduce variability and bias in human judgments, since users may have different expectations or cultural perspectives when judging responses. Additionally, pairwise comparisons require large amounts of user participation to generate statistically reliable rankings. Another limitation is that the evaluation primarily focuses on conversational quality and user preference, which may not fully measure other important aspects such as factual accuracy, safety, or reasoning ability.

Future research could explore ways to combine crowdsourced human evaluations with automated metrics or AI-based judges to create more comprehensive evaluation frameworks. Researchers may also investigate methods to reduce bias in preference data, improve statistical ranking techniques, and expand evaluation to include specialized domains such as scientific reasoning or coding. Another promising direction is integrating dynamic or continuously updated benchmarks that evolve as models improve. Together, these approaches could lead to more robust and reliable methods for evaluating increasingly powerful language models.
