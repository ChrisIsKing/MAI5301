Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models

The paper “Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models” talks about a major problem with current large language model (LLM) inference: most LLMs make decisions based on tokens from left to right, which makes it hard for them to do complex reasoning that needs planning, lookahead, or strategic decision-making. This limitation is important since many real-world tasks, like solving puzzles, writing creatively, or playing strategy games, need you to think about a lot of different actions and pick a globally good order of choices instead of merely continuing the text in one place. Traditional inference methods can become caught on bad reasoning paths and not find good or right answers.

Previous research has presented prompting techniques such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT), which directs the model to provide intermediate reasoning steps that facilitate the attainment of a conclusive result. CoT enhances performance on numerous reasoning tasks by revealing latent reasoning; yet, it produces a singular linear sequence of tokens, missing tools for branching, exploring alternatives, or backtracking. Some extensions, like self-consistency, sample several chains separately, although they usually don't include systematic investigation or evaluation of intermediate pathways. Consequently, the current level of related research is predominantly limited to linear reasoning frameworks or simplistic sampling techniques that fail to replicate the more systematic search methodologies characteristic of classical AI.

The authors suggest the Tree of Thoughts (ToT) framework, which is a general way to make decisions by looking at several "thoughts" coherent language segments that show intermediate problem states arranged in a tree form. ToT doesn't make a single chain of tokens; instead, it creates a search tree of possible thoughts over and over again. Every node in the tree stands for a piece of the solution or a phase in the reasoning process. At each node, the model comes up with several viable next thoughts. It then assesses these candidates in relation to the problem aim and picks the best ones to work on further. This enables lookahead, backtracking, and global planning processes comparable to systematic search engines in conventional AI. ToT uses the language model itself not just to create new ideas, but also to judge the worth of partial solutions. This makes it possible to employ search heuristics within the language modelling framework.

The authors provide empirical evidence that ToT markedly enhances performance on tasks necessitating complex planning or search relative to traditional urging methods. For instance, in the Game of 24, GPT-4 with vanilla chain-of-thought prompting only answered around 4% of the problems, but ToT solved about 74%. Similar improvements can be shown in creative writing and tiny crossword puzzles. This shows that explicitly looking at and judging different ways of thinking can make you significantly better at solving problems.

The ToT strategy has problems, even though it has some good points. The most important thing is the cost of computation. To explore a tree of reasoning routes, you need to travel through the language model many more times than you do to make a single chain. This makes ToT less useful for real-time or resource-limited apps because it increases latency, API charges, and battery use. The approach also needs appropriate heuristics to make and judge candidate thoughts. If the heuristics are bad, the search space could get too big or the paths could be ranked wrong.

For future study, promising avenues encompass the development of more efficient search techniques that integrate ToT using reinforcement learning or external knowledge stores, creating learned evaluation functions to improve search guidance, including ToT with tool usage (such as external solvers or calculators), and modifying ToT for more complex multimodal tasks. There is potential for theoretical investigations into constraining search complexity or extending ToT to additional model classes beyond the existing autoregressive LLMs.

Let’s Verify Step by Step

The study "Let's Verify Step by Step" looks into a basic challenge in teaching large language models (LLMs) to reason correctly about complex activities that involve many steps, especially maths. Even the most advanced models can sometimes make logically wrong intermediate steps or "hallucinated" reasoning, even when they sometimes give the right end solution. This makes people less likely to trust their ability to reason and makes it harder to use them in important areas like education, science, and decision support. The primary issue examined is the optimal method for supervising multi-step reasoning during training to minimise logical errors: should supervision concentrate just on the ultimate result (outcome monitoring) or additionally encompass each intermediate reasoning step (process supervision)? This topic is significant since LLMs need to be able to reason well in order to be safe and reliable while doing complicated jobs, and getting feedback from people is expensive, therefore it's necessary to use input wisely.

Most of the previous work on LLM reasoning has been on outcome supervision, which just gives feedback on whether the final result is accurate, or prompting approaches like chain-of-thought reasoning, which show intermediate steps without supervising them. Some previous comparisons (e.g., Uesato et al., 2022) indicated that process oversight would be beneficial, however substantial empirical data was absent. Conventional reward models designed for Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) primarily assessed final replies, potentially reinforcing erroneous reasoning techniques that nonetheless yield right results. This makes it hard to know how well and how quickly different monitoring tactics work for teaching models that can reason well.

To tackle this issue, the authors systematically compare result supervision with process supervision using a demanding subset of the arithmetic dataset, which includes complex arithmetic problems that need several logical steps to solve. They explicitly define process supervision as giving detailed human feedback that labels each step in a reasoning trail as positive (correct and helpful), neutral, or negative (incorrect or unimportant). The authors employ this step-level input to train a Process Reward Model (PRM) that gives scores to incomplete reasoning sequences at each step. This model is then used to help the machine develop through reinforcement learning. They also look at active learning, which is when the model finds cases it is most unsure about and asks for feedback from people. This helps focus expensive annotations where they are most needed.

The main idea behind their technique is that learning to reason step by step and judging each stage leads to far better reasoning than only focusing on the end result. This is because process supervision helps the model find and fix logical errors early on, learn how to think logically, and apply what it has learned to new situations more effectively. The process-supervised model empirically resolves 78% of issues from a sample selection of MATH tests, significantly surpassing outcome supervision benchmarks. They also show that active learning makes process oversight even better by lowering the cost of annotation and increasing accuracy. They give PRM800K, a dataset containing 800,000 human-labeled step-level feedback annotations, to help with more study.

But the method has certain problems. First, getting step-level human annotations is hard work and costs a lot compared to result supervision. Active learning is helpful, but it still needs a lot of annotation work, which could not work in all fields. Second, the process-supervised models mostly deal with mathematical reasoning. It's not apparent how this method works for other types of reasoning problems or multimodal thinking that doesn't use text. Lastly, PRMs that were trained on specific datasets, like MATH, may only work in that field and not be able to be used in other fields without more annotation.

Future research should focus on the automated or semi-automated generation of process supervision signals to minimise annotation costs, the expansion of process supervision frameworks to additional reasoning domains (such as scientific and commonsense reasoning), the integration of external structured reasoning modules, and the investigation of the interaction between PRMs and broader context and memory architectures. Exploring enhanced active learning methodologies and multi-domain generalisation of process supervision may augment reasoning reliability at scale.
