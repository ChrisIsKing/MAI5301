**On the Measure of Intelligence**

The paper On the Measure of Intelligence by Francois Chollet looks at a basic but important question in AI research, which is what we really mean when we say a system is intelligent. The author explains that many current AI systems are judged by how well they perform on specific tasks, such as image recognition or games. However, doing well on a single benchmark does not necessarily mean the system can adapt or learn in new situations. Chollet argues that skill can be increased just by giving a model more data or computation, so high performance alone should not be confused with intelligence.

In the paper, intelligence is defined as the efficiency with which an agent can learn new skills across different tasks. The discussion is mainly theoretical. The author builds his definition using ideas connected to algorithmic information theory and clearly separates the role of prior knowledge, experience, and the difficulty of generalization. He then outlines what a proper intelligence benchmark should look like and introduces the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus as an example. ARC is designed to test abstract reasoning using limited prior knowledge, in a way that is closer to how humans approach new problems.

From my understanding, the main point is that intelligence is about how well and how quickly something can learn, not just how much it already knows. A strength of the paper is its clear argument and careful breakdown of existing evaluation problems. A weakness is that the claims are mostly conceptual, and the benchmark is very difficult, so its full impact is still uncertain. Overall, the goals of redefining intelligence measurement are mostly achieved.
