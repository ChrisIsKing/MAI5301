Paper 1: "GPTs are GPTs: An Early Look at the Labor Market Impact Potential of Large Language Models"
Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock (2023)
Problem and Importance
The paper investigates the potential implications of large language models (LLMs) on the U.S. labor market, focusing in particular on the increased capabilities arising from LLM-powered software compared to LLMs on their own. This is an important problem because prior waves of automation robots and earlier machine learning systems mostly threatened routine, manual, and lower-wage jobs. LLMs appear to invert this pattern: their fluency in language, reasoning, coding, and knowledge synthesis makes them most relevant to knowledge-intensive, higher-wage occupations, raising novel and urgent questions about economic inequality, workforce disruption, and the pace of structural change.
Related Work
The paper builds on a rich tradition of labor economics research on technological displacement. Prior frameworks include the "suitable for machine learning" rubric of Brynjolfsson & Mitchell (2017), the AI occupational exposure scores of Felten et al. (2018), and the automation risk estimates of Frey & Osborne (2017), which primarily predicted that lower-wage, routine-manual jobs faced the greatest displacement risk. The authors note that the variance explained by these earlier efforts, along with wage controls, ranges from 60 to 72%, indicating that 28 to 40% of the variation in their AI exposure measure remains unaccounted for by previous technology exposure measures demonstrating that LLMs introduce genuinely new dimensions of labor exposure not captured by existing instruments.
Proposed Solution and Key Insight
The authors devise a novel task-level exposure rubric applied to O*NET's detailed work activity database, assessed by both trained human annotators and GPT-4 itself. The key conceptual insight is the distinction between LLMs alone and LLM-powered software ecosystems. With access to an LLM, about 15% of all worker tasks in the U.S. could be completed significantly faster at the same level of quality. When incorporating software and tooling built on top of LLMs, this share increases to between 47 and 56% of all tasks. The headline finding is that around 80% of the U.S. workforce could have at least 10% of their work tasks affected by the introduction of LLMs, while approximately 19% of workers may see at least 50% of their tasks impacted. Critically, and counterintuitively, the influence spans all wage levels, with higher-income jobs potentially facing greater exposure. The paper concludes that LLMs qualify as general-purpose technologies (GPTs) pervasive, improving over time, and generating co-invention spillovers thereby justifying the paper's clever double entendre in its title.
Limitations
The central limitation is that the rubric measures exposure potential, not actual adoption, displacement, or productivity effects. Emergent capabilities, human perception biases, and technological development shifts can all affect the accuracy and reliability of predictions regarding LLMs' potential impact on worker tasks. Projections are inherently forward-looking and based on current trends, evidence, and perceptions of technological possibilities. Additionally, the analysis covers only U.S. occupations via O*NET, limits its scope to text-based tasks, excludes vision and physical dexterity requirements, and relies heavily on annotators who may not be domain experts in every occupation being evaluated.
Future Directions
The paper itself calls for research into adoption timelines, the design of complementary tools, and policy responses. Beyond that, natural extensions include longitudinal empirical studies measuring actual productivity and wage changes after LLM adoption, cross-country comparisons to assess how different labor-market institutions shape outcomes, and decomposing exposure by gender, race, and educational attainment to anticipate distributional consequences. Research into how workers can be trained to collaborate with LLMs rather than simply compete with them also represents an important open frontier.


Paper 2: "Future of Work with AI Agents: Auditing Automation and Augmentation Potential across the U.S. Workforce"
Shao, Zope, Jiang, Pei, Nguyen, Brynjolfsson & Yang (2025)
Problem and Importance
The rapid rise of compound AI systems AI agents is reshaping the labor market, raising concerns about job displacement, diminished human agency, and overreliance on automation. Yet, there is a lack of systematic understanding of the evolving landscape. Prior work on LLM labor market exposure (e.g., Eloundou et al.) focused on static, text-in/text-out capabilities. AI agents, by contrast, can plan, use tools, browse the web, write and run code, and execute multi-step workflows a qualitatively different technological profile. Crucially, the workers most affected by this shift have largely been absent from the research conversation: existing studies assess capability but rarely ask workers what they actually want automated. This gap is both empirically and ethically significant, since deploying AI agents without worker consent risks eroding human agency and workplace wellbeing.
Related Work
The paper situates itself at the intersection of two streams. On the AI side, agent benchmarks such as GAIA, AgentBench, and OSWorld assess autonomous task completion, but such approaches, while useful for capability evaluation, fail to provide a holistic and worker-centric understanding of how these agents could be integrated into the broader workforce. On the labor economics side, the O*NET-based exposure paradigm pioneered by Eloundou et al. and others is the standard foundation, but these studies rely entirely on expert judgment and ignore worker preferences.
Proposed Solution and Key Insight
The paper introduces a novel auditing framework and constructs the WORKBank database. The framework uses an audio-enhanced mini-interview to capture nuanced worker desires and introduces the Human Agency Scale (HAS) as a shared language to quantify the preferred level of human involvement. Data were collected between January and May 2025, yielding assessments from 1,500 domain workers and 52 AI experts covering 844 tasks across 104 occupations. By jointly considering worker desire and expert capability assessments, the framework partitions tasks into four zones: an Automation "Green Light" Zone (workers want it, capability exists), an Automation "Red Light" Zone (workers resist, even if capability exists), an R&D Opportunity Zone (workers want it but capability lags), and a Low Priority Zone. Domain workers generally express positive attitudes toward AI agent automation, particularly for repetitive and low-value tasks. The HAS profiles reveal a dominant inverted-U trend across occupations, indicating that workers generally prefer human involvement at the mid-stages of complex workflows not pure automation, and not pure manual work. The paper also finds early signals that AI agent integration may shift valued competencies from information-processing to interpersonal skills.
Limitations
Workers may lack full awareness of the evolving capabilities and limitations of AI agents, potentially shaping their responses. Some workers may also withhold honest feedback due to concerns about job security or surveillance. Moreover, the dataset covers only 104 of the hundreds of occupations in O*NET, is focused on the U.S. workforce, and relies on crowdsourcing platforms that may not perfectly represent the diversity of actual workers in each role. The framework also does not yet account for how worker preferences might evolve as they gain hands-on experience with AI agents.
Future Directions
The WORKBank database is explicitly designed to be extensible, and the authors envision tracking how worker preferences and agent capabilities co-evolve over time. Promising directions include longitudinal studies to observe preference changes post-deployment, cross-cultural extensions to examine how worker desires differ across labor markets, and participatory design research that actively involves workers in co-designing AI agent systems. The skill-shift signal (from information to interpersonal competencies) also warrants urgent attention in workforce development and education policy.


Paper 3: "Levels of Autonomy for AI Agents"
Feng, McDonald & Zhang (2025)
Problem and Importance
Autonomy is a double-edged sword for AI agents, simultaneously unlocking transformative possibilities and serious risks. The central problem addressed in this paper is the absence of a principled design vocabulary for calibrating how much autonomy an AI agent should exercise. As agents proliferate from OpenAI's Operator to Cognition's Devin to Google's Deep Research developers tend to treat autonomy as an incidental output of raw capability: the more capable the agent, the more it is assumed to act independently. This creates serious hazards. Fully autonomous agents can perform scams, leak private data, cause human deskilling, and erode societal agency in ways that are difficult to attribute or remedy. The problem is important because, without structured autonomy levels, neither developers, regulators, nor users have a shared vocabulary for assessing, communicating, or governing agent behavior.
Related Work
The paper draws on human-robot interaction research, which has long distinguished levels of automation along a human-machine authority spectrum (e.g., Sheridan's Levels of Automation). It also engages the emerging AI safety literature, including Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, which classifies agents with even "low-level autonomous capabilities" as significantly higher-risk. Related work on human-AI collaboration (e.g., Brynjolfsson & Mitchell's "co-pilot" paradigm) and multi-agent systems engineering provides additional context, but the current evaluation paradigm of using benchmarks narrowly focuses on autonomous task completion accuracy a framing the paper explicitly challenges.
Proposed Solution and Key Insight
The paper defines five levels of escalating agent autonomy, characterized by the roles a user can take when interacting with an agent: operator, collaborator, consultant, approver, and observer. The key insight is that autonomy is a design decision, not a capability epiphenomenon a highly capable agent can still be constrained to operate at L1 (user as operator) through architectural choices and interface design. At L1, the agent provides on-demand assistance without executing actions without explicit user approval; at L2, agent and user actively co-plan and co-execute; at L3, the agent takes initiative but consults the user at key junctures; at L4, the user approves outputs but is otherwise uninvolved; and at L5, the user is a passive observer. The paper then proposes autonomy certificates credentials issued by a governing body that communicate an agent's behavioral autonomy level to other developers, users, and multi-agent systems, analogous to safety certifications in other engineering domains. It also outlines early ideas for autonomy-specific evaluation designs distinct from capability benchmarks.
Limitations
The framework is primarily conceptual and has not yet been empirically validated. The five-level taxonomy, while intuitive, borrows its structure from the SAE autonomous vehicle levels a context with clear physical safety stakes and it remains unclear whether this structure cleanly maps onto the diverse and often ambiguous workflows where AI agents are deployed. The autonomy certificate proposal is also aspirational: it assumes the existence of a trusted, neutral third-party governing body whose design, authority, and enforcement mechanisms are not addressed. Furthermore, the framework focuses on the user-agent dyad and does not deeply address the principal hierarchy problem (developers, deployers, end-users) that complicates real-world governance.
Future Directions
The paper itself calls for empirical studies evaluating whether users can reliably identify and appropriately trust agents operating at different autonomy levels. Broader future work includes designing adaptive autonomy systems that fluidly adjust autonomy level based on task stakes, user expertise, and error signals; exploring how the framework extends to agentic pipelines where agents are users of other agents; and developing regulatory infrastructure analogous to financial or pharmaceutical regulation to operationalize autonomy certificates at scale. The relationship between autonomy levels and accountability (who is responsible when an L4 or L5 agent causes harm?) is also a rich open legal and ethical question demanding interdisciplinary attention.
