Switch Transformers: Scaling to Trillion Parameter Models with Simple and Efficient Sparsity
The paper addresses a central challenge in modern deep learning: how to build and train extremely large transformer models efficiently. Dense transformer architectures, where every parameter in the model is used for every input token, scale poorly as model size increases, leading to excessive computational cost and memory usage. This limits practical training and deployment of state-of-the-art models that could otherwise reach trillions of parameters. Sparse model designs such as Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) have been explored as a way to activate only parts of a network per input, reducing computation. However, prior MoE approaches suffered from complex routing strategies, high communication overhead in distributed training, and instability during optimization. These limitations made large sparse models difficult to use in practice despite their theoretical efficiency advantages.
At the time of this work, most successful large language models (e.g., T5, GPT-3) were dense, requiring all weights for every token and thus demanding enormous compute resources. Earlier sparse models pointed toward improvements but introduced their own challenges: many-to-many expert routing schemes that were costly to coordinate across devices and difficult to scale without prohibitive overhead. Against this backdrop, the authors propose a significantly simplified MoE architecture called the Switch Transformer, which streamlines these ideas into a more practical and scalable form.
The key innovation of the Switch Transformer is its simple routing mechanism: instead of activating multiple experts per token, each token is routed to only one expert in a given MoE layer. This design greatly reduces the amount of communication required across computational devices and lowers the per-example computational burden. Because each token interacts with a single expert, the model can contain many more parameters overall without increasing the effective compute per token, enabling efficient scaling to trillion-parameter models. In addition, the authors introduce techniques to stabilize training, such as managing expert capacity and using lower-precision data types like bfloat16. When integrated into well-known Transformer variants (e.g., T5-Base and T5-Large), the Switch Transformer achieves major speedups in pre-training—up to 7× faster compared to equivalent dense models, and early instances of trillion-parameter sparse models that outperform much larger dense counterparts while requiring roughly the same computation budget.
Despite these advances, the Switch Transformer has limitations. Simplifying routing to a single active expert per token can reduce representation richness compared with systems that allow multiple experts, potentially limiting performance on highly complex tasks. Sparse models also require substantial engineering and large hardware clusters to train effectively, keeping them out of reach for many research groups and companies without extensive computational resources. Finally, while training efficiency is improved, fine-tuning and downstream task performance can demand careful adjustment of routing and expert balancing strategies.
Looking ahead, future research directions include developing more sophisticated routing mechanisms that preserve efficiency while enabling richer expert coordination, improving methods for adaptation to diverse downstream tasks, and exploring hybrid sparse-dense architectures that balance performance and compute more effectively. Efforts to make sparse models more accessible and energy-efficient may also broaden their adoption and impact in both research and practical applications.


Mixtral of Experts
This paper introduces Mixtral 8x7B, a novel Sparse Mixture of Experts (SMoE) large language model designed to dramatically improve model performance and efficiency by combining a sparse expert architecture with state-of-the-art training practices. The central problem this work addresses is the difficulty of scaling language models to high performance without prohibitive compute costs. Traditional dense models such as Llama 2 70B or GPT-3.5 use all parameters for every token processed, which results in high inference cost and resource requirements. Sparse models, in contrast, aim to activate only a subset of parameters per token, allowing the model to “scale” its effective capacity without a proportional increase in active compute. This is important because achieving competitive performance on benchmarks involving mathematics, reasoning, multilingual tasks, and code generation requires models with high capacity, but such capacity traditionally demands expensive computational infrastructure. Mixtral 8x7B addresses this efficiency-scaling tradeoff by enabling very large total parameter counts while keeping active inference costs manageable. 
Prior work on sparse expert models broadly includes approaches like Mixture of Experts and more recent systems such as Switch Transformers, GShard, and other SMoE architectures that enable conditional computation by routing tokens to only a few experts. These models showed that it is possible to reduce per-token compute while maintaining or improving overall performance, but earlier designs often involved complex routing, inefficient hardware utilization, or limited public access. Dense models like GPT-3 or Llama 2, despite their success, illustrate the compute and energy costs of dense parameter usage, and sparse methods represent an ongoing research direction to make large models more practical. The state of related work thus blends dense transformer baselines with emerging sparse techniques that aim for better compute-performance tradeoffs. 
The core innovation of Mixtral 8x7B lies in how it structures and uses its expert networks. Architecturally, the model builds on the decoder-only Transformer design used in Mistral 7B but replaces the single feedforward layer in each block with a set of 8 distinct feedforward experts. At each layer and for each token, a lightweight router network selects two experts, computes their outputs, and combines them. Because only two experts are used per token, the model only engages about 13 billion active parameters during inference, even though the full model contains ~47 billion parameters that it could potentially employ. This sparse selection enables efficient computation while still providing access to a much larger parameter space. Mixtral’s training also uses a 32 k token context window and integrates fine-tuning techniques like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) for its instruct-tuned variant. To evaluate performance, the authors compare Mixtral against dense models like Llama 2 70B and GPT-3.5 on a suite of language understanding and generation benchmarks, showing that Mixtral often matches or surpasses these larger dense models on metrics for math, code, multilingual understanding, and reasoning. Additionally, a fine-tuned version called Mixtral 8x7B-Instruct demonstrates competitive human preference performance against commercial chat models. Both models are released under the Apache 2.0 license, encouraging open research and deployment. 
Despite these achievements, the Mixtral architecture has limitations. One drawback is that the SMoE structure introduces additional routing overhead and memory usage patterns that can be less efficient on standard hardware compared with dense models, especially in low-batch or latency-critical settings. Because inference involves selecting and combining expert outputs dynamically, this can lead to irregular memory access patterns and complexity in serving infrastructure. Moreover, sparse models can be harder to optimize and tune during training, particularly in ensuring balanced expert utilization and avoiding “expert collapse” where some experts are under-utilized. The requirement for specialized batching or expert parallelism strategies also poses challenges for deployment in resource-constrained environments. 
Future research directions include improving the efficiency and stability of sparse expert routing mechanisms, investigating dynamic expert addition or pruning to adapt model capacity on the fly, and exploring hybrid models that combine dense and sparse layers to balance performance and hardware utilization. Moreover, better tooling and frameworks that streamline SMoE deployment could broaden access beyond large research labs. Extending the context length capabilities further and integrating multimodal inputs (e.g., vision and language) in a sparse expert paradigm are additional exciting avenues. Finally, understanding how these sparse architectures generalize in real-world applications and ensuring fairness and safety in larger scale deployments remain crucial topics.
