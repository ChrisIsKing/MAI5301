The paper Holistic Evaluation of Language Models addresses the growing challenge of how to rigorously and transparently evaluate large language models (LLMs). As LLMs have become increasingly influential in both research and real-world applications, evaluation practices have not kept pace with their rapid development. Most existing evaluations focus narrowly on task accuracy using a small number of benchmarks, while overlooking other critical properties such as fairness, bias, robustness, safety, efficiency, and calibration. This narrow focus is problematic because it can mask serious shortcomings in deployed systems, leading to unsafe, biased, or misleading model behavior. A more comprehensive evaluation approach is therefore essential for responsible model development, meaningful comparison across models, and informed deployment decisions.
Prior work in this area has largely relied on isolated benchmarks or single-metric evaluations, such as accuracy on question answering or language understanding datasets. While some studies have examined individual aspects like bias or robustness, these efforts are often fragmented and lack consistency across models and tasks. As a result, comparisons between language models are difficult to interpret, and it is unclear which models are truly better when broader real-world considerations are taken into account. There has been no widely adopted framework that systematically defines evaluation scenarios, metrics, and standards in a unified manner.
To address these gaps, the authors propose HELM (Holistic Evaluation of Language Models), a comprehensive framework designed to standardize and broaden the evaluation of language models. The key insight behind HELM is that evaluation should begin with an explicit definition of what matters, rather than being driven solely by available benchmarks. The framework introduces a top-down taxonomy that specifies realistic usage scenarios alongside multiple evaluation metrics for each scenario, including accuracy, calibration, robustness, bias, toxicity, fairness, and efficiency. By applying these metrics consistently across a wide range of models, HELM enables meaningful comparisons and highlights trade-offs that are often hidden in single-metric evaluations. The authors demonstrate this approach by evaluating numerous language models across 16 core scenarios and 7 metrics, resulting in a rich, multidimensional view of model performance.
Despite its strengths, the HELM framework has limitations. It cannot fully capture all possible tasks, languages, or social contexts, and some domains and populations remain under-represented. Additionally, the framework does not prescribe how different metrics should be weighted, which means stakeholders with different priorities may draw different conclusions from the same results. Maintaining the relevance of HELM over time also poses a challenge, as language models and their applications evolve rapidly and require continuous updates to evaluation scenarios and metrics.
Future research can build on HELM by expanding the range of scenarios and metrics, particularly for emerging applications such as interactive agents and multimodal systems. Greater emphasis on low-resource languages and culturally diverse contexts would improve inclusivity and fairness in evaluation. Further work is also needed on automated and adaptive evaluation methods, as well as deeper assessment of alignment, ethical reasoning, and long-term safety in deployed language models.
 
The paper Measuring Massive Multitask Language Understanding introduces an extensive benchmark designed to evaluate how well large language models (LLMs) understand and reason across a wide array of tasks. As language models have grown dramatically in scale and capability, researchers noticed that many standard benchmarks were no longer sufficiently challenging: models began achieving near-superhuman scores on datasets like GLUE and SuperGLUE, despite still struggling with broader comprehension and reasoning that humans perform naturally. This reveals an evaluation gap—models might excel on narrow tasks without truly understanding language holistically. The authors argue that to assess progress toward more general intelligence, we need benchmarks that capture breadth across subjects and domains, because without them, researchers cannot reliably judge how close models are to human-level understanding. 
Prior to this work, evaluation of language models tended to focus on individual tasks or small sets of curated benchmarks. Classic datasets like GLUE and SuperGLUE aggregated a handful of NLP tasks, but improvements on those benchmarks no longer correlated well with real-world competency as model capabilities increased. Other evaluations examined specific competencies such as reading comprehension or commonsense reasoning, but none attempted to measure massive multitask performance across many disciplines. As a result, existing evaluations could miss critical weaknesses—models might perform well in certain areas while failing catastrophically in others, especially in tasks requiring domain knowledge or reasoning across fields. 
To address this gap, the authors propose a new comprehensive benchmark called Measuring Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU). This benchmark consists of 57 multiple-choice tasks spanning diverse academic subjects such as mathematics, history, computer science, law, and ethics, among others. Each task is structured so that high performance requires deep knowledge and reasoning ability across multiple domains. Critically, the test includes topics that are socially significant and conceptually complex, such as morality and law, which are often near random-chance for many models. By evaluating LLMs on this wide range of tasks and measuring average accuracy across subjects, the benchmark reveals not just task skill but the breadth of understanding a model possesses. 
Empirical evaluations using this benchmark showed that while early large models like GPT-3 (175B parameters) significantly outperform a random baseline (approximately 25% accuracy), they still fall far short of expert-level performance on nearly all tasks, often only slightly above chance. Models also exhibit lopsided performance: they may do reasonably in one category but perform poorly in others, and they often fail to recognize when their answers are incorrect. This uneven competence underscores that high performance on narrow benchmarks doesn’t necessarily translate to broad, general knowledge or reasoning ability. 
Despite its contributions, the MMLU benchmark has limitations. It relies on multiple-choice questions, which may constrain the range of reasoning assessed and could allow models to exploit answer patterns rather than demonstrate deeper comprehension. Additionally, the benchmark may not fully capture conversational or generative language capabilities, focusing instead on discrete question answering. Over time, as model capabilities continue to rise, the benchmark itself may need extensions or harder tasks to remain challenging. 
For future research, expanding this benchmark to include more complex reasoning types, open-ended responses, and tasks requiring multi-step explanation could provide richer evaluation of language understanding. There is also room for exploring adaptive benchmarking that grows in difficulty as model performance improves, as well as cross-linguistic extensions to assess global language understanding beyond English. Finally, integrating evaluation of safety, ethical reasoning, and uncertainty estimation could make benchmarks more aligned with real-world expectations for responsible AI systems.
